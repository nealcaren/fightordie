## Priority Revisions

### 1. Add Du Bois's voice through direct quotation

**What:** Insert 4–6 direct quotations from Du Bois at key moments throughout the chapter.
**Why:** Zero quote density in an introduction to a writer's collected works. The reader never encounters the voice the chapter describes as influential.
**How:** Select passages that demonstrate Du Bois's editorial range — one polemical, one lyrical, one programmatic. Place the first quotation in the opening paragraph or immediately after it. Use the collected articles as the source. Frame each quote with a single sentence of context before and a sentence of interpretation after.
**Model:** Not directly modeled in the 22-book corpus (which introduces arguments, not archival collections), but the principle of grounding claims in primary evidence applies universally.

### 2. Write a real opening that establishes stakes

**What:** Expand the opening from 1 paragraph (~70 words) to 2–3 paragraphs (300–500 words).
**Why:** Universal move 1 (opening hook) rated Weak. The current opening reads as a reference entry, not as an invitation to explore a significant collection.
**How:** Open with a striking Du Bois quotation or a specific scene from *The Crisis*'s history (e.g., the record-selling May 1919 issue, or the moment Du Bois saw the first printed copy). Follow with a paragraph establishing what made *The Crisis* distinctive — not just that it existed, but what it did that nothing else could. End the opening section by previewing the arc: from founding ambition to forced departure.
**Model:** Hybrid Navigator opening pattern — scene or historical moment with paradox (300–800 words).

### 3. Add a thesis or framing argument

**What:** Insert 1–2 paragraphs after the opening that state what the chapter (and the collection) argue or reveal.
**Why:** Universal move 3 (core argument) rated Missing. The chapter narrates events but never tells the reader what to make of them.
**How:** The thesis for an edited volume introduction is different from a monograph thesis. It should answer: "What does reading these articles together reveal that reading them individually does not?" Possible framings: (a) The arc from integration to separatism as a response to institutional failure. (b) *The Crisis* as an experiment in whether a publication could function as both organizational tool and independent intellectual forum. (c) Du Bois's editorial evolution as a window into the tensions within the early civil rights movement.
**Model:** Adapted from the Hybrid Navigator THESIS_STATE function — clear enough to quote, but framing a collection rather than proving a causal claim.

### 4. Write a closing that synthesizes and claims stakes

**What:** Expand the closing from 1 paragraph (~90 words) to 2–3 paragraphs (300–500 words).
**Why:** Universal move 6 (stakes claim) rated Weak. The current closing is too compressed to carry the weight of a 6,200-word chapter.
**How:** The closing should do three things: (a) Synthesize the arc — what changed between 1910 and 1934, and what that trajectory reveals. (b) Connect to the present — not with a vague "the issues have not gone away" but with specific contemporary resonances. (c) Orient the reader to the collection — what they will find, what themes to watch for, how the articles are organized.
**Model:** STAKES_CLOSE paragraph function — the "so what" that sends the reader into the collection.

### 5. Compress the Moon/Horizon section

**What:** Cut the Moon/Horizon section (lines 21–69) from ~1,900 words to ~800–1,000 words.
**Why:** This section takes 31% of the chapter to cover precursor publications that are not part of the collection. The African American press landscape paragraphs (lines 27–39) are the most encyclopedic material in the chapter.
**How:** Cut lines 27–39 (the general landscape of African American journalism) entirely or compress to 2–3 sentences. Reduce The Moon coverage from 4 paragraphs to 2. Keep the Horizon material that directly prefigures *The Crisis*'s editorial approach. Reallocate the saved space to the opening, closing, and a roadmap of the collection's contents.

### 6. Add a guide to the collection

**What:** Insert a section (or expand the closing) that orients the reader to the volume's contents.
**Why:** Universal move 5 (structural roadmap) rated Missing. The reader finishes the introduction without knowing what articles are collected, how they are organized, or what to read first.
**How:** This is the edited-volume equivalent of a chapter roadmap. Describe the collection's organization (chronological? thematic?). Highlight 3–5 articles that are particularly significant. Tell the reader what to notice across the collection — recurring themes, evolving positions, rhetorical strategies.

### 7. Integrate sources into the prose

**What:** Add in-text citations at key interpretive claims throughout the chapter.
**Why:** The bibliography lists 12 scholarly works, but none appear in the text. The chapter makes numerous historical claims without attribution.
**How:** Add parenthetical citations where the chapter draws on specific sources. Prioritize Lewis (1993, 2000) for biographical claims, Rudwick (1958) for editorial history, and Alexander (2014) for *The Crisis*'s broader significance. This need not be heavy — 10–15 citations would transform the chapter from popular exposition to scholarly introduction.
